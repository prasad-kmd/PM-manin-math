# Mathematical Visualization Rules

## Mathematical integrity

- Verify the mathematics before designing the animation.
- Do not distort a graph, diagram, scale, or geometric relationship for visual convenience without explicitly indicating the distortion.
- Use accurate axes, units, domains, ranges, labels, and assumptions when they matter.
- Distinguish an illustrative example from a general proof or theorem.
- Avoid implying that a numerical experiment proves a general mathematical statement.

## Equations and derivations

- Present a derivation in meaningful steps.
- Keep unchanged terms visually stable when moving between expressions.
- Highlight the operation or term currently under discussion.
- Do not reveal a final expression before the reasoning unless the scene intentionally previews the result.
- Ensure that transformations are algebraically valid and that any assumptions are stated.

## Graphs and coordinate systems

- Include axes, ticks, units, and labels only to the extent that they help the explanation.
- Choose plot ranges that reveal the relevant behavior without hiding important features.
- Use consistent scales when comparing graphs unless a different scale is clearly identified.
- Keep the curve visually distinct from axes and guide lines.
- Animate parameters or points in a way that accurately reflects the mathematical definition.
- Avoid unnecessary grid lines and tick labels.

## Geometry

- Construct diagrams with accurate relationships whenever they are presented as mathematical constructions.
- Use consistent point labels and avoid placing labels on top of vertices or lines.
- Distinguish construction lines from the main figure through quieter color or stroke weight.
- When a diagram is schematic rather than to scale, do not imply exact measurements from its appearance.
- Make the relationship being explained visually prominent.

## Vectors and linear algebra

- Use consistent colors for vectors and their components.
- Make arrow direction and magnitude clear.
- Keep coordinate axes visually secondary.
- When demonstrating transformations, distinguish the original object, transformed object, and any reference frame.
- Do not change basis, scale, or coordinate convention without showing or stating the change.

## Calculus and dynamic processes

- Use curves, tangent lines, secants, shaded regions, accumulation, or moving points only when they clarify the concept.
- When illustrating a limit, show the relevant quantity approaching its target rather than implying that a finite animation proves the limit.
- For derivatives, make the relation between local change and the tangent slope clear.
- For integrals, explain the relevant interval and the meaning of the accumulated quantity.
- For differential equations, distinguish a solution curve, direction field, and numerical approximation when applicable.

## Probability, statistics, and data

- Represent quantities proportionally when the visual suggests a quantitative comparison.
- Label sample spaces, axes, distributions, and parameters when needed.
- Do not use misleading scales or hide uncertainty.
- If displaying simulated data, distinguish it from exact theoretical results.

## Topic adaptation

Select visualization techniques based on the actual topic and intended learning outcome. Do not force every topic into a graph or geometric animation. Some concepts are better explained through symbolic transformations, comparisons, counting, recursion, or carefully staged examples.
